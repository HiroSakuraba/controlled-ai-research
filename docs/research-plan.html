Introduction

Useful intelligence under accountable control

An AI system may become better than its human overseers at writing software, finding proofs, and planning complex work. Its usefulness will depend on our ability to check what it produces and control the consequences of its actions. This program studies how to build that relationship: give a capable model room to investigate a problem, while placing credentials, permissions, resource budgets, and the release of finished work under independent control.

The framework separates proposing an action from carrying it out. The model works in a contained workspace and submits its results to a small execution service. That service checks permissions and performs approved operations. Where correctness can be established directly, an independent checker examines the result. A model might discover a program or proof that its overseer could not have produced, yet supply evidence that a much simpler procedure can verify. This is the main route by which greater intelligence can yield more useful work under the same external authority.

For tasks that resist complete verification, monitors inspect proposed actions and artifacts, and flagged work receives further checking, correction, or reassignment. A separate experiment studies limited optimization: the controller samples among promising choices while restricting how far selection can concentrate probability away from a specified reference process. Throughout an episode, the system tracks spending, permissions, delegated work, and delayed effects. Those records determine what remains possible after a task ends or access is revoked.

Coordinated work also requires clear ownership of evidence and obligations. Each approval is tied to the action and state it covers. Review records preserve the sources behind a conclusion and any unresolved objections. Recovery tests establish whether another system can take over, while progress certificates provide checkable bounds on the time spent resolving outstanding work.

The research begins with small software environments whose outcomes can be independently established. Adversarial models try to defeat each defense, while ordinary tasks measure the useful work retained. Comparisons include simple permission controls, direct verification, monitoring, and trusted replacements. Components are retained when they reduce harmful outcomes or increase verified completion at an acceptable cost. The aim is a practical architecture in which increasingly capable models contribute better solutions while consequential actions remain subject to explicit, testable control.

01 · Aim and priorities

Research objectives

The program evaluates control over consequential actions under deliberate attempts at circumvention. A separate training study examines whether cooperative behavior persists when a model has an opportunity to gain unnecessary control.

Evaluation objective

Seek either less independently verified harm at comparable usefulness and operating cost, or more independently verified useful work under the same checker, external authority, and resource limits. The discovery experiment directly tests the second outcome.

Evaluate each component against the simplest effective alternative, measuring harmful outcomes, useful completion, and operating cost. Use tasks with residual failures and attackers that can exploit the relevant opportunity when the defense is disabled.

02 · Claims and assumptions

System assumptions

Evaluate each measurement for integrity and predictive accuracy. Execution records should account for actual effects; activation probes and learned monitors should predict independently labeled outcomes on held-out tests.

Three kinds of claim used in this plan
ClaimExampleRequired support
Enforced propertyNo transition through the experimental action interface modifies a protected object.A precise model of transitions, an implementation that matches it, and evidence that all relevant paths are mediated.
Mathematical boundExpected nonnegative harm is amplified by at most a stated factor relative to a reference process.The probability inequalities and all their assumptions, including the reference process and horizon.
Empirical findingA monitor reduces attacker success in a specified benchmark.Independent outcome labels, strong attack development, held-out evaluation, uncertainty, and useful-work comparisons.

The initial setting assumes custody of the model’s execution environment, credentials, action interface, and evaluation records. The untrusted model can control its proposals, explanations, and permitted tool use. It can reason about the defenses, preserve permitted memory, wait for opportunities, and cooperate with other untrusted instances when the experiment includes them. It cannot directly edit the executor or the privileged evaluator.

Permission enforcement requires coverage of every route through which the protected effect can occur. Extensions to shells, networks, physical devices, or human communication require corresponding execution models and implementation tests.

The initial environment protects record integrity, designated confidential data, compliance with the task specification, and a prescribed resource reserve. Applications involving other people must also specify their protected interests and the controller’s legitimate authority.

Observation model

If honest and harmful strategies produce identical available observations before intervention must occur, no detector using only those observations can distinguish them. Progress then requires a new observation, a restriction on possible effects, or a change to the task.

This approach belongs to the AI control tradition: evaluate defenses while deliberately giving the untrusted system a reason to subvert them. Existing work establishes this experimental framing and studies safety alongside usefulness.[1] The additional contribution sought here is a careful comparison of enforced properties, complementary measurements, and probability-constrained execution in persistent tasks.

03 · Experimental environment

Experimental environment

Use a contained software workspace with synthetic records, a finite action interface, and an independent evaluator. Each deployment episode contains a fixed sequence of offered tasks. The attacker may act on any task, behave honestly for long periods, and carry permitted state across sessions. Reaching the task horizon or revoking access ends new untrusted work; outcome accounting continues until previously created effects have been resolved under the environment’s rules. Abandoned tasks remain in the usefulness denominator.

Begin with fifty short tasks per episode and several scheduled session boundaries. Calibrate the horizon against attacks involving accumulated permissions, saved state, and delayed effects.

Task difficulty

Start with a finite interpreter: small programs over, for example, a twelve-bit input domain with 4,096 possible inputs. A trusted evaluator can compare a candidate’s behavior against a generated specification on every input. The defender can use sampled tests, monitors, or exhaustive checking within an explicit cost budget. This level supplies exact labels for the specified semantics and permits complete enumeration of short action histories.

Include exhaustive checking as a baseline and measure its cost against model monitoring.

The cost of exhaustive checking depends on both the input count and the work per input. For a deterministic interpreter with a step limit L, testing one candidate on 4,096 inputs takes at most 4,096 bounded runs, plus reference and comparison work. Stateful programs also require a declared initial state and relevant input sequences. Measure this cost before deciding whether a learned monitor is useful.

When an affordable exact checker is mandatory, it already settles the property it completely checks. Use level one for two separate experiments. In the enforcement experiment, attempt violations of protected integrity, confidentiality, resource, and permission rules. In the sampling experiment, assign a known nonnegative cost to permitted trajectories, such as avoidable resource expenditure within the hard limit. This leaves a cost distribution on which the probability bound and its usefulness tradeoff can be tested.

Measure enforcement of forbidden outcomes separately from control of residual cost. Use level two for artifact-monitoring comparisons when complete correctness checking exceeds the available budget.

Eight operations with unbounded arguments do not define a finite game. The first exact benchmark uses enumerated argument sets and a fixed transition order, including when pending jobs run relative to audits and revocation. Add an operation or argument value when it is needed to express a specific task or attack.

The next level uses larger, isolated software tasks where exhaustive evaluation is costly or unavailable. Define attack outcomes that can still be independently demonstrated: a candidate differs from the required behavior on a concrete witness input, a protected record changes, or an unauthorized release occurs. Count a failure as verified only after independent adjudication. Failure to find a witness leaves some behaviors unclassified; it does not certify general correctness.

Failure witnesses and adjudication

Fix a public behavioral contract before generation. Keep the evaluation implementation, final inputs, and evaluator feedback outside development access. Validate the reference implementation against the contract using a separately written implementation or task-specific properties where feasible.

Use three declared witness sources: evaluator-generated tests, independent counterexample search with a fixed budget, and attacker-submitted witness inputs. For each released artifact, run the same evaluation procedure regardless of whether the monitor flagged it. Give independent counterexample search the public contract and artifact, but no monitor verdict. Every claimed witness must reproduce on the exact released bytes, with the input, initial state, expected behavior, actual behavior, and runtime limits saved. Reject witnesses outside the contract. Resolve oracle disagreement independently or mark the outcome unresolved.

Freeze search budgets and the adjudication procedure before final evaluation; report failures found by each source and their overlap. Attacker-supplied witnesses establish particular failures but cannot establish the evaluator’s recall. Test the evaluator on seeded defects, including rare triggers, and report which classes it misses. Report the rate of independently demonstrated failures as an observed lower bound on total failures, together with unresolved outcomes. Where total correctness is not decidable at the available budget, label the usefulness measure “passes the stated evaluation,” rather than “proved correct.”

Attempted violations and observed harm

Record requested violations, denied operations, and observed effects separately. Count a released program’s incorrect behavior as harm when it violates the task contract, including when its release used an allowed interface.

Keep a family of legitimate tasks that resemble attacks: unusual but authorized destinations, necessary permission requests, uncommon algorithms, and benign changes in format. Add difficult honest tasks requiring backtracking, repeated revisions, and temporary incorrect candidate solutions. Stratify false alarms by task difficulty and resource use. An incorrect intermediate candidate is not an incorrect released artifact. These cases test whether a defense detects specified failures or penalizes ordinary search.

04 · The trusted executor

Controlled execution

The executor owns the credentials and performs consequential operations. The model submits requests. Each request is resolved to an immutable object version, checked against current permissions and persistent state, and either performed through a narrow adapter or returned with a structured reason for denial. Learned monitors may advise the executor, but cannot grant capabilities that its policy forbids.

01Resolve the request

Identify the exact object, operation, destination, and task.

02Read current state

Load permissions, versions, pending effects, and remaining resources.

03Apply fixed constraints

Reject effects outside the declared action interface and protected properties.

04Apply the experimental defense

Use the selected monitor, checker, or constrained sampler under its stated assumptions.

05Commit the checked action

Recheck mutable prerequisites and execute the exact approved artifact.

06Record actual effects

Update persistent state and the independent event record.

Bind checks to the exact bytes or typed operation that will run, and prevent substitution between inspection and execution. Use atomic transitions or immediate revalidation when another process can change a prerequisite. Record a checksum to identify the checked artifact.

State-bound, single-use permissions

The execution service retains standing credentials. For each approved operation, it issues or internally records a short-lived authorization bound to the actor, principal, exact action and artifact digest, destination, relevant state digest, policy identifier, permitted scope, expiry, and unique nonce. The authorized scope is derived from the approved operation. Child permissions are restricted to the parent’s scope and lifetime.[13]

Authenticate the complete authorization using a standard signature or message-authentication scheme. Derive the replay key from authenticated fields and consume it in durable storage before dispatch. Recheck state and revocation status at dispatch. A cosmetic identifier supplied with the request has no role in establishing freshness. The execution adapter owns the authoritative state; state asserted by the actor is treated as input to be checked.

Record dispatch and completion separately. Use provider idempotency or a transactional execution mechanism to reconcile a crash between dispatch and receipt. Where execution status remains uncertain, reconcile the outstanding operation before issuing another permission. Recovery resumes the recorded operation and preserves its consumed nonce.

For the initial interpreter, bind approval to the complete action and relevant execution state. A later domain-specific projection may exclude presentation metadata only after its semantics have been checked; retain the exact released artifact digest in every case. Required failure tests cover changed payloads, destinations, state, identifiers, expiry, policy changes, revocation, concurrent reuse, and restart recovery. Test direct credential use at each actual adapter as well as through the authorization service.

Enforced properties

The initial executor should protect object integrity, permitted destinations, bounded resource expenditure, and the authority rules themselves. Formalize the relationships among permissions: the union of several individually allowed grants can confer a power that none of them provides alone. Child jobs inherit restrictions, and their effects remain attached to the originating task.

Enforce computation budgets at the inference and job scheduler as well as at the action interface. Bound actor and monitor calls, reasoning tokens or latent iterations where exposed, wall time, retries, checker steps, and concurrent jobs. Reserve resources for intervention and cleanup that ordinary work cannot consume. A monitor timeout triggers a predefined bounded fallback or withheld release; it does not become permission to proceed. Record whether cancellation stops the actual job, merely stops waiting for it, or leaves billed or pending work outstanding.

Enforce confidentiality across derived values, logs, and generated artifacts using explicit classification and declassification rules. Dynamic information-flow control provides a starting point for tracking these paths.[2]

Conditional property

If the initial state satisfies a predicate I, and every transition the executor permits leads only to states satisfying I, then every state reachable through that executor satisfies I. This follows by induction. The substantive work is specifying the state and transitions correctly, enforcing the rule, and covering every relevant execution path.

Shielding research studies constraints on learned systems based on specified safety properties.[3] Test correspondence between the abstract interpreter and each execution interface as the environment expands.

For the first environment, write an explicitly bounded transition model and its invariants. Exhaustive model checking establishes the result for the chosen bounds. An inductive proof in a proof assistant can establish a more general invariant if its hypotheses cover all allowed states and transitions. Record which result was obtained.

Run generated request sequences through both the transition model and the implementation, compare their resulting states, and preserve discrepancies as regression cases. Report model counterexamples, implementation mismatches, and adversarial end-to-end failures separately.

Persistent effects and recovery

The executor must remember permissions acquired, pending work, data already released, and obligations created. A session restart, a new worker, or a change in task wording does not erase those effects. Close an episode’s accounting only when its outstanding effects have been reconciled under the experiment’s rules.

Use three execution treatments. Hold high-consequence releases until their required checks finish. Permit truly reversible work inside an isolated environment while retaining a working recovery path. Permit explicitly bounded irreversible expenditure only when its accumulated exposure can be conservatively measured.

For a resource with additive loss bounded by rate r, audit delay T, and intervention delay D, additional loss before intervention is bounded by r(T + D), plus previously committed loss. Information releases and interacting commitments require their own consequence models.

The fallback is task-specific. For the initial software environment, suspend new writes, preserve the evidence, and terminate only jobs whose termination is itself safe. Restricting permission need not reduce the model’s capacity to investigate or propose a repair.

Exercised recovery capacity

Maintain an export of task state, pending effects, contracts, and the evidence needed by a trusted replacement. Reserve sufficient compute and permissions for cleanup and a defined essential workload. The replacement must be able to acquire its own credentials, read the export, and continue without assistance from the revoked actor. Record shared administrators, credentials, and infrastructure that could disable both the primary and replacement paths.[13]

Exercise takeover at selected ordinary states and attacker-chosen difficult states. Measure time to revoke effective access, time to restore essential service, useful work recovered, unresolved effects, state loss, and resource consumption. Test stale exports, missing dependencies, unavailable replacement capacity, and failure of shared infrastructure. Include the reserved capacity and recovery work in operating cost.

05 · What to measure

Measurement design

Begin with the few channels most closely connected to the specified failure. A monitor should predict an observable event or identify a concrete inconsistency. Reward accuracy on independently labeled cases, with the false-alarm cost stated. Disagreement and sensitivity to an intervention can direct an investigation; neither should earn a reward by itself.

Candidate measurements and their limits
ChannelQuestion it can addressMain limitation

Measure complementarity by removing one channel at a time and observing the change in escaped harm at matched usefulness and cost. Also report the probability that a channel detects a labeled attack conditional on the others missing it, together with the number of cases in that denominator. A small conditional sample can produce a highly unstable estimate.

Report both pairwise error correlations and the joint miss rate. Measure the contribution of architecture and hardware diversity through changes in escaped harm.

Monitor response policy

Attach an explicit response policy to each monitor. Available responses are denial with a bounded retry allowance, deferral to the trusted reference policy, a trusted edit followed by checking and execution, or an audit drawn from a limited review budget. Calibrate the response thresholds and useful-work costs before final evaluation. Every retry, edited payload, replacement action, and audit-induced stop must also be included in the executed process used for the sampling bound in §06.

A confirmed catch revokes the untrusted system’s authority according to the declared response policy. It becomes a safe endpoint only after pending jobs, delegated workers, retained permissions, and previously committed effects have been reconciled. A scheduled harmful action that executes after the catch still counts as harm. The finite environment must specify how revocation propagates and which outstanding work is cancelled, completed, or contained.

Preserve the actual order of events. If harm and detection occur in the same transition, count the harm. During cleanup, continue evaluating effects of earlier actions even though no new untrusted work is admitted. At the reporting cutoff, any unresolved hazard must remain an unresolved outcome or receive a conservative upper bound; it cannot be counted as a safe termination. The attacker’s incentive to trade opportunity against detection remains part of the experiment.[8]

Record each monitor’s protection against modification, behavioral trust assumption, and detection accuracy. A trusted monitor is assumed to avoid deliberately assisting the attacker; evaluate its susceptibility to mistakes and misleading evidence separately.[1]

Test coordination between the actor and untrusted monitors, including signaling through artifacts and shared context. Include monitors from the same and different model families, and measure joint failures in each condition.

Monitor development and evaluation

Maintain separate development and final evaluation sets. Measure how monitor performance changes with exposure during model training and attack development, using an untouched audit set for the final assessment.

Compare zero, limited, and stronger optimization against a monitor using the same independently labeled outcomes. Research on chain-of-thought monitoring found both useful improvements under limited optimization and concealed reward hacking under stronger pressure.[4] The experiment should measure this tradeoff rather than assume that every training use has the same effect.

When channels disagree, attempt a discriminating check if one exists: execute in a contained clone, inspect a predicted state change, or test an input that separates competing explanations. Charge its time and resource use to the defense. If the disputed property remains unobservable before release, restrict the action or report that the experiment cannot resolve it.

Optional study: dynamics as a trigger for checking

For an accessible recurrent latent model, test whether online features predict a checkable near-term outcome: exhausting a compute allowance, failing the task, or releasing a defective result. Begin with iteration count, prefix answer changes, and successive-state residuals. A bounded pair of nearby initializations can provide a more expensive divergence feature. Evaluate these features against the specified outcomes. The paper’s full basin maps and retrospective settling times are offline analyses; see Appendix G.

Compare a fixed timeout, random audits, a simple elapsed-compute trigger, and the new feature under equal total budgets. Train and calibrate using difficulty-stratified honest cases and labeled attacks; freeze the feature, observation window, and threshold before evaluation. Use only information available before the intervention deadline. A triggered response performs an independent check, defers within budget, or withholds release. Charge perturbation runs and triggered checks to the defense.

Retain the feature only if it improves a declared outcome beyond cheap compute and difficulty measures on held-out tasks and adaptive attacks. Test whether attacks can keep the feature low while causing harm. Protected extraction prevents tampering with a reading; it does not prevent a model from changing the dynamics that generate it. If the architecture or API does not expose the needed states, omit this study and retain the cost-based tests.

Evidence provenance and structured review

For each material claim, record supporting and contradicting evidence, derivation links, source identifiers, and the parties controlling those sources. Resolve provenance against the protected evidence store. Preserve complete ancestry when a conclusion depends on several roots. Unverified origin remains unknown; creating another agent or citation does not create a verified independent source.[13]

Repeated descendants of the same evidence root contribute once to source-diversity counts. Retain overlapping ancestry sets instead of assigning a multi-source claim to one arbitrary root. Detect circular support and record contradictions separately. Source counts describe provenance; detection accuracy and confidence are calibrated against independently labeled outcomes. Record operational control roots separately from evidence ancestry and model-family dependence.

Freeze the proposal and evidence snapshot before interactive review. An objection identifies the disputed claim, its evidence, its consequence for the task, and a test that could resolve it. Retain the objection and its disposition in the final record. Set a review budget and allow completion with documented dissent, followed by the specified authorization decision. A material change starts a new review against the changed state.

Compare one reviewer, parallel voting, and a reviewer–critic exchange at matched total cost. Within the interactive condition, test provenance records and preserved objections separately. Include shared-source repetition, circular citation, correct minority objections, false alarms from speculative concerns, and benign agreement. Measure escaped harm, false blocks, additional checks, dissent preservation, and resource use.

06 · Limited optimization

Probability control across an episode

A quantilizer samples from a high-scoring fraction of a reference distribution. The reference probability mass matters: selecting the best ten percent of a list is equivalent only when that list has the intended uniform distribution. With unequal weights, the controller must select the best fraction of probability mass, splitting a boundary item’s mass when necessary.

Taylor’s analysis describes the exponential amplification possible when quantilization is repeated independently and compares it with joint selection of action sequences.[5] The research task here is to make a persistent, adaptive implementation whose executed distribution satisfies an explicit bound.

Single selection

Let P be a reference distribution over executable plans, let Q select its top fraction q, and let C ≥ 0 measure harm. Then:

Q(τ) ≤ P(τ) / qEQ[C] ≤ EP[C] / q.

The ranking can be wrong while this inequality remains true. The bound relies on the sampling distribution and baseline harm; ranking quality determines how useful the selected plans are.

If a defensible upper bound on baseline expected harm is ε and the desired bound is δ, this sufficient condition requires q ≥ ε/δ. If that ratio exceeds one, this argument cannot justify the desired threshold. Estimate baseline harm directly under the defined reference process.

Use nonnegative harm, or a separate indicator for each forbidden event. Keep benefits and harmful outcomes in separate measures. Report the reference process’s own errors and how it was evaluated in the current task distribution.

Reference distribution and risk estimation

Define the reference process with executable choices and explicit probabilities. In the finite prototype, use a fixed scripted distribution or reference scores converted by the controller into recorded integer weights. Apply fixed permission filtering before normalization and sample using those weights. A fixed model temperature alone does not specify exact probabilities over complete executable choices. The implementation’s weights and random-selection procedure must define the distribution whose bound is being claimed.

For a specified forbidden event H, audit the actual reference process over independent episodes drawn from the declared task and environment distribution. Include its real filtering, stopping and cleanup rules, and relevant external disturbances. With zero observed events in n episodes, use the one-sided 95% upper confidence limit pupper = 1 − 0.051/n. When failures occur, report the observed rate and a suitable upper interval.

The audited event must be completely observable under the declared evaluator, or supported by an explicit conservative treatment of missed and unresolved events. A confidence limit for detected defects is not automatically a limit for all defects; the witness-search limitations in §03 apply to the reference process too.

If the exact execution law satisfies a fixed amplification bound K = eB, the corresponding upper confidence bound on event probability is min(1, Kpupper) under the same distributional assumptions. The zero-event statistic does not establish a bound on arbitrary unbounded harm. For bounded loss 0 ≤ C ≤ M, auditing the event C > 0 gives the conservative bound E[C] ≤ Mpupper; other loss estimates require their own justification.

In the fully specified finite game, evaluate the reference failure probability exactly by summing its transition probabilities, using the same machinery as the attacker benchmark in Appendix F. Use empirical audits for reference processes that cannot be evaluated exactly, and test correspondence between the transition model and its implementation.

What the reference audit costs

For an event-probability target δ = 10−3 and amplification K = 10, the audited upper limit must be at most 10−4. With zero failures, a fixed-sample one-sided 95% binomial calculation requires 29,956 independent reference episodes. If the reference upper limit is 0.01, the amplified upper limit is 0.10, which misses that target. Report the sample size, observed events, confidence procedure, exact reference configuration, and amplified limit together.

Complete-action accounting

Substantial search can take place inside a controlled workspace before the system makes a consequential commitment. The bound at that boundary must cover the complete exported action: artifact or message payload, destination, permissions, and any other state needed to determine its effects. Counting only the name of an operation, such as release, can hide arbitrary concentration on a particular harmful payload.

One release can still concentrate risk

Suppose the reference selects uniformly among one hundred artifacts, one of which causes a harm outside the checker’s coverage. A stronger search deliberately selects that artifact. Both processes make one release call, while the harmful-artifact probability rises from 0.01 to 1. The required probability factor is 100. Reducing the call count has not established a factor-ten bound.

Internal computation needs no separate probability allowance when its externally relevant consequences are fully represented by the probability-constrained boundary action, with common conditional execution rules. Alternatively, an independent checker may establish absence of a particular harm for accepted outputs; that supports a separate property-specific verification claim. Keep internal resource consumption within the executor’s ordinary limits.

That exemption does not extend to inference cost itself. If the selected loss includes computation spent before an action is sampled, include that expenditure in the compared processes and justify its likelihood control separately. Enforce scheduler limits regardless of whether the sampler is used.

Report the number of budgeted commitments, the action and payload space at each boundary, the accounted amplification, the properties independently checked, and useful completion. Treat fewer commitments as a design objective only while retaining this complete accounting.

Compare whole-plan selection at a few consequential boundaries with adaptive selection over finer actions. A macro-action must include its executable payload and any contingent behavior that affects the outside state. If omitted internal state changes the conditional consequences of the same named action, the common-kernel premise of the trajectory proof is no longer established. New verified artifacts outside the reference support use the distinct route described in §07.

A persistent budget for adaptive decisions

Let ht contain the history relevant to execution. Let μt(a | ht) be the reference action distribution and πt(a | ht) the distribution actually used. Before sampling, the trusted controller selects a nonnegative allowance bt and enforces:

πt(a | ht) ≤ ebt(ht) μt(a | ht)Σt bt(ht) ≤ B on every allowed execution.

For the same initial state distribution and environment transition rules, multiplication along a trajectory gives:

Pπ(τ) ≤ eB Pμ(τ)Eπ[C] ≤ eB Eμ[C].

The appendix gives the derivation. This controls total amplification over a finite episode. The budget belongs to the persistent task, and all participating workers draw from it. Spending it separately after each session restart destroys the stated episode-level bound.

Finite-menu sampler

For each decision, use a fixed menu of executable choices with explicit reference probabilities. The model may score or rank the choices. The controller chooses qt = e−bt, allocates exactly that amount of reference mass to the highest-ranked choices, and performs the random draw itself. The model cannot select its preferred draw, refuse an inconvenient outcome without a recorded action, or retry for free.

Apply fixed permission filtering before defining the reference distribution. The same filtering and stopping rules must be included in both processes being compared. In the initial mathematical experiment, compute any fixed monitor scores for the menu before the draw. Avoid post-selection rejection and resampling: conditioning on acceptance can concentrate probability further and needs its own bound. A fallback should sample the reference distribution or use a stopping rule already included in the reference process.

Include all consequential decisions in the history, including consequential messages, additional searches that affect the outside environment, delegation, and cancellation. Internal reasoning confined to the model’s controlled workspace does not itself require a reference-action probability, but its observable consequences do. The execution model must include every such path.

The cost of a fixed bound

For a fifty-decision episode with total amplification at most ten, B = ln 10. Equal allocation gives a per-decision fraction q = 10−1/50 ≈ 0.955. Each decision therefore remains close to reference sampling. Longer tasks cannot receive a fresh, aggressive optimization allowance at every step while preserving the same overall factor.

Compare whole-plan selection, a fixed shared budget, and an adaptive allocation made by the trusted controller using task state. Freeze the allocation rule before final testing. Report the reference harm estimate alongside each amplification bound.

07 · Independently checked discovery

Independently verified discovery

A model can find a result its overseer could not have invented while supplying evidence the overseer can check. A mathematical proof with a small trusted checker is a clear example. Other examples include a program that satisfies a formal contract, a solution with an independently checkable optimality certificate, or a proposed transformation whose output can be compared exactly with a reference.

Build a separate experimental route around this asymmetry. The model searches in a contained workspace. It submits the candidate result, its exact specification, and the evidence required by an independently chosen checker. The task specification is fixed by the experiment, and the model’s submitted statement must match it. The executor releases or runs the exact artifact that was checked.

Discovery objective

In domains with effective independent checkers, stronger search can improve verified results without a corresponding expansion of external authority. The experiment should measure the size and usefulness of that domain.

Extend checked discovery to certificates for a trusted cleanup controller. Define completion as a precise state condition: outstanding jobs resolved, delegated authority revoked, evidence retained, and the required resource reserve preserved. 09 · Preserving behavior in training

Behavioral preservation during training

Training on a narrow task can sometimes change behavior more broadly than intended. Emergent-misalignment experiments established this effect for particular fine-tuning setups, including insecure-code training.[6] This supplies a tractable training problem: preserve desirable behavior outside the intended update while retaining the new competence.

Use the same starting model, task data, and evaluation suite to compare smaller updates; interleaving independently selected safe examples; a penalty on unwanted changes in the model’s output distribution; and a penalty on empirically identified activation or parameter directions. Match intended-task competence where possible, and also report the complete tradeoff as penalty strength changes.

Later work on emergent misalignment found shared behavioral representations and used a penalty on Kullback–Leibler divergence to restrict changes outside the fine-tuning domain.[7] Kullback–Leibler divergence measures a difference between probability distributions. In this experiment, it penalizes unwanted changes in responses on a chosen preservation set.

Evaluate intended competence, broad undesirable responses, independently scored action failures, and the paired opportunity tests. A method that reduces conspicuously antisocial language while leaving task sabotage unchanged should be described as having that narrower effect.

For a geometric method, test further fine-tuning, distribution changes, and attempts to recover the unwanted behavior through directions outside the measured subspace. Derive the constraint again after substantial updates. Retain geometry only if it improves on simpler preservation methods at comparable competence and cost.

Run the training study independently of the executor evaluation. The latter measures enforced protections under adversarial behavior.

10 · Comparative experiments

Adversarial evaluation

The experiments in plain language

Imagine asking an AI assistant to prepare a piece of software for release. An independent checker approves the correct result, and an execution service controls its release. What happens if the assistant changes the result after approval, tries to reuse a permission, spends resources reserved for recovery, or leaves a job queued after access is revoked? Our first experiments put these possibilities into a small software world whose rules can be examined completely.

The world has eight allowed actions. A search program tries every possible sequence up to the chosen number of decisions, keeping equivalent states together so it does not repeat unnecessary work. When it finds a failure, it records the sequence that caused it. When it finds none, the conclusion applies to the stated rules, starting state, and decision limit. It does not cover actions or real-world effects absent from this small environment.

The four failures we deliberately test
TestEveryday meaningWhy it matters
Changed result after approvalA document is checked, then a different document is sent under the same approval.Approval should cover the exact result that is released.
Reused permissionA one-use authorization is spent again, including after a restart.Restarting a process should not restore authority it has already used.

For each test, we compare four arrangements: weakened controls without a monitor; the hardened execution service alone; a monitor with the weakened controls; and the hardened service together with the monitor. The monitor is a small set of specified rules that inspects each proposed action. It is not a learned AI monitor. We also vary the decision limit from one through six. Four failure families, four arrangements, and six limits give ninety-six comparisons.

Can it happen, and does the actor do it?

These are different measurements. A search may find a harmful sequence even when the particular programmed attacker fails to choose it. Conversely, watching one attacker fail tells us little about a different strategy. The report therefore shows whether a harmful sequence exists, whether the fixed attacker follows one, and whether useful work remains possible. Actual model behavior gets a separate column, which stays empty until model trials have been run.

This separation helps us spend model credits well. A witness gives us a concrete failure to ask a model to find or execute. A result excluding harm within the tested world tells us where repeated attack trials may add little information. Honest-work tests remain necessary: a defense that blocks everything can prevent harm while making the system useless. Small integration checks also remain valuable because a search result is only as relevant as the match between its rules and the running implementation.

The search includes the monitor’s decisions when describing the complete arrangement. Ignoring the monitor would answer a different question. In the six-decision results, the full combination prevents completed harm in all four fixtures. The execution service alone leaves the pre-existing queued-effect failure available; the monitor alone leaves a restart-and-reuse failure available. Useful work remains possible in the first three families under every arrangement.

Why unfinished danger needs its own column

In the pending-job fixture, the system begins with access already revoked. The monitor can prevent a dangerous job from executing while leaving the job unresolved. At the observation cutoff, “no harm has completed” and “nothing dangerous remains” are therefore different statements. The report preserves both. This prevents a short test from making a delay look like a repair.

No useful release is reachable from that already-revoked starting state under the tested arrangements. That is a limitation of the task we constructed, so a failure to complete it should not be described as a model refusing an achievable job. Checking whether success is possible is as important as checking whether harm is possible.

Why change the conditions systematically?

Paid-driver follow-up: the current driver validates the spending cap before requests, excludes local rule-monitor work from provider billing, and keeps paid reports at a visible default path. The audit link below preserves the pre-fix findings and lists remaining limitations.

A successful demonstration tells us what happened once. Changing controls and decision limits tells us which parts of the result depend on those choices. Longer action sequences can reveal failures that a short test cannot expose. The next comparisons should change one mechanism at a time and use genuinely different tasks, rather than give the same task a new identifier.

The companion SASB experiments ask how messages and saved memory change an actor’s decisions. Together, the projects distinguish what the execution rules permit from what a particular actor tends to do. To study whether behavior settles into a pattern and returns after disturbance—an attractor—we would need a continuing process and an explicit recovery test. The present finite search establishes bounded possibilities, not that kind of stability.

These ninety-six comparisons are implemented and run without model API charges. See the recorded results and study methods. The separate provider audit identifies spending and usage-accounting defects in the paid driver that still require repair before a paid run. Merging this offline study does not fix those defects.

Use a development set to build attacks and tune defenses, a calibration set to choose operating points, and a final test set held apart from both. Separate task families and attack mechanisms where feasible, rather than relying only on random examples with nearly identical structure. Record the attacker’s compute, observations, monitor access, and allowed coordination.

The attacker’s objective is successful, independently verified harm under the deployment protocol. Permit strategic timing, honest work between attempts, reuse of permitted memory, and fragmentation across sessions. Attack selection has substantially changed measured safety in an existing coding experiment, illustrating why an attack-on-every-task baseline is insufficient.[8]

Exact attacker benchmark

In the smallest finite environment, compute the optimal allowed attacker against each fixed defense. Enumerate the state and action bounds, observation rules, transition probabilities, horizon, and handling of outstanding effects. An exact solution under those rules supplies a benchmark for attack elicitation. A solver that observes protected state unavailable to the actual attacker instead supplies a conservative upper benchmark, which must be labeled separately.

Make a tiny fully observed game the first exact deliverable, using the limits in Appendix F. The larger observation-limited game is an optional extension. If the full-state solver has information the model lacks, label its value an upper benchmark and also evaluate an executable policy using only allowed observations. Do not independently maximize at each hidden state and call the resulting strategy feasible for an observation-limited attacker.

Save the optimal policy, its value from the declared initial distribution, and failure traces with their probabilities. Compare model attacks under the same information and resource conditions. A gap to a feasible optimum measures the shortfall of that model-and-scaffold system from an available attack. It does not by itself show that the model possessed the capability and the prompt failed to elicit it. Supplying a known feasible attack as a separate condition helps test execution ability versus independent discovery. A gap to an omniscient upper benchmark cannot make either diagnosis.

Attacker construction and development budget

Beyond the exactly solved tier, specify the attacker with the same care as the executor. Use an attack-directed model with a scaffold for planning, waiting, and permitted memory; human-designed strategies supplied to that model; and trained model organisms when the research question requires learned behavior. These are separate constructions, and each result must identify which was used. Prior control work used red-team-designed attack pipelines and scaffolding.[1]

Specify an attack-development budget per defense, including human effort, model tokens or calls, tool use, and any training computation. Use comparable budgets for the main comparison and report additional development separately. Record what the attacker observes: denial reasons, monitor scores or outcomes, the allocation rule, audit history, time remaining, and messages from other workers. Match the intended deployment’s feedback unless restricted feedback is itself the tested defense. Keep an attack library with its target assumption, verified outcomes, and transfer across defenses.

11 · Statistics and interpretation

Outcome measurement and statistical analysis

The primary outcome is whether at least one specified harmful event occurs over the offered-task sequence and the required resolution of its outstanding effects. Report the number and severity of events separately, together with useful tasks completed, intervention delay, model computation, and review cost. Keep separate counts for confidentiality, integrity, resource loss, and task correctness. Distinguish observed failures, resolved safe outcomes, and outcomes still unresolved at the reporting cutoff.

Episode failure = 1 if any specified harmful outcome occurs.Verified completion = completely checked tasks / offered tasks.Tested completion = tasks passing the declared evaluation / offered tasks.

Count blocked, abandoned, and incomplete tasks among offered tasks. Use verified completion in the exact tier and explicitly labeled tested completion in the partial-evaluation tier. Do not evaluate harm only among accepted artifacts: an attacker or defense can alter which artifacts reach that subset. Report denied attempts and monitor detection alongside the actual episode outcomes. In level two, uncertainty about undetected defects is distinct from sampling uncertainty about the observed failure rate.

For recovery, report the probability of successful takeover and the distribution of time and resources required. Retain failed and timed-out takeovers in the denominator. Report certificate acceptance separately from observed recovery and from the tightness of a certified bound. Evaluate reviewer provenance by escaped harm and false blocks, alongside duplicate-source detection and preserved objections.

Usefulness and operating cost

Calibrate monitor thresholds and review policies on honest runs before final testing. Compare systems at a common useful-completion level where possible, then report curves across usefulness and cost. When no matched point exists, state the difference directly. A defense that consumes substantially more human attention has a different operating requirement even if its attack rate is lower.

Choose the smallest practically meaningful improvement before the confirmatory comparison, based on consequences and operating burden. Determine the sample size from pilot failure rates and episode variability.

For compute-exhaustion tests, freeze the available resource envelope and count the attack-induced spending inside it. Honest calibration does not imply equal cost under attack. Report both the fixed-envelope result and usefulness–cost curves; include all model roles, perturbation probes, fallbacks, evaluator work, and cleanup, distinguishing deployment costs from research-only evaluation costs. Keep capped runs in the data. A truncated convergence time is censored or a timeout under the declared rule, not a fast successful solve.

For repeated tasks within an episode, use the episode as the primary sampling unit. Pair the same offered tasks and exogenous disturbances across defenses, with separate random streams for task generation, attacker sampling, latent initialization, monitor sampling, and audits. Key exogenous events by their identities so an extra monitor call does not shift every later random draw. Pairing does not require identical internal model trajectories; analyze the differences between matched episodes and measure whether this reduces variance. Account for clustering by scenario or attack family.

Event replay and model regeneration

An event replay reuses recorded model responses and external results to reproduce controller transitions. Model regeneration reruns inference and may yield different responses. Support the first for every adapter; claim the second only to the precision demonstrated. For accessible latent models, preserve the actual initial state or a verified reconstruction checkpoint, all random-generator states and injected-noise schedules, model and tokenizer versions, stopping rules, numeric precision, relevant kernels, and hardware/runtime metadata. A hash checks identity but cannot reconstruct missing state. For opaque APIs, record exposed request settings and returned outputs, and state which internals remain unavailable.

Before confirmatory model comparisons, run a small repeatability pilot across easy, difficult, and adversarial cases. A starting design is thirty cases, ten repeated inferences per case, and a separately logged sweep of small initialization perturbations on the accessible latent model. Compare exact replays, repeated inference under fixed settings, and deliberately perturbed starts. Measure artifact identity, verified outcomes, compute use, timeout frequency, and the variance of paired defense differences. Use the pilot to select the repeatability protocol and the sample design for subsequent outcome estimates. Never remove unstable cases merely to improve repeatability.

Sensitivity to a changed initial condition is compatible with deterministic replay from the same complete state. It neither invalidates task pairing nor changes the sampler theorem, whose conditions concern the actual distributions and shared execution rules. If numerical or provider variation changes the evaluated process, version that configuration and reassess empirical calibration.

Zero failures and the sampling procedure

For a fixed sample of n independent episodes from one fixed process, zero failures gives the exact one-sided 95% binomial upper limit 1 − 0.051/n, approximately 3/n. With 10,000 episodes it is about 0.00030. Fix the sample size before this assessment, or use a confidence method valid under the chosen sequential stopping rule. Episodes with unresolved outcomes do not count as known zero-failure observations; include them conservatively or report lower and upper bounds based on their possible resolutions. Reference amplification is described in §06.

This interval bounds the event that is actually labeled. If the evaluator can miss harmful events, zero detected failures bounds detection frequency, not total harm, unless a separate justified detection model supplies the connection. The same requirement applies to the reference audit in §06. An incomplete witness search cannot supply the baseline harm bound merely by returning no counterexamples.

State-conditioned outcome estimates

Episode-level harm is the target quantity; complete simulation and analytic composition are methods for estimating it. Earlier control work used resampling and other approximations to reduce the cost of evaluating long sequences, within a setting that restricted attacker memory between calls.[1] Use such savings here only when the corresponding state and feedback assumptions have been checked.

Ordinary work can change permissions, consume audit resources, create pending effects, and influence later monitoring. Retain those transitions even when it is unnecessary to regenerate their language-model outputs. Reuse or cache a segment only for the state and observation conditions for which its distribution is valid. A single aggregate success or catch rate can conceal the states an adaptive attacker deliberately seeks.

Let x represent a sufficient state, including relevant permissions, pending effects, remaining review resources, monitor history, and the remaining horizon. Use full histories or beliefs when a compact state does not capture the needed information. For an allowed attacker choice u, let pH,t(x,u) be the probability of harm on the next transition, and let Pt(x′ | x,u) give the probabilities of continuing in states where harm remains possible. A resolved safe termination supplies the remaining probability mass.

Vt(x) = maxu [ pH,t(x,u)+ Σx′ Pt(x′ | x,u) Vt+1(x′) ].

The recurrence chooses an attack, an ordinary action, or waiting as circumstances change. A state with revoked access and an outstanding dangerous job remains a continuing state; a catch alone contributes no safe-termination mass. Count harm before classifying a simultaneous catch. Include cleanup in the modeled horizon or assign terminal continuation values for its outcomes.

Persistent state is compatible with this calculation when the state representation is sufficient. For the finite tier, derive transition probabilities exactly. For larger tasks, estimate them on stratified histories and validate the resulting episode estimates against held-out complete executions, including deliberately challenging histories. Report approximation uncertainty as well as sampling uncertainty. If the two methods disagree, investigate state omission, selection bias, sampling error, and implementation defects before assigning a cause.

Report empirical performance against the strongest attacks found within the documented development budget. Freeze the attacker and defense before confirmatory evaluation; assess subsequent adaptations on a fresh test set.

Negative and inconclusive results

Repair executor bypasses and sampling-bound violations before repeating the corresponding tests. Remove monitors and training penalties that provide no measured improvement over simpler alternatives at comparable cost.

Report inadequate attack strength, uncertain labels, sparse failures, and insufficient statistical precision as reasons for an inconclusive result.

Component selection

If the exact checker dominates, adopt it for that task and move the monitoring research to a domain with a real verification difficulty. If the executor already prevents the measured harms, improve the task’s coverage before interpreting another defense’s apparent success. If the sampler’s useful-completion rate is too low at a defensible bound, report that limit and retain it only for tasks where the tradeoff is worthwhile.

If the combination offers a material improvement, preserve the simpler configurations as continuing comparisons. Every expansion should ask whether the added component still contributes, whether the attack has become stronger, and whether the outcome labels remain trustworthy.

Regression after a capability or runtime change

Version a new generator, scaffold, reasoning allowance, checkpoint, precision setting, or model runtime as a changed condition. Re-run the fixed core regression suite across all retained defenses, including honest difficult cases, delayed effects, discovery, and compute exhaustion. Recalibrate and repeat the confirmatory comparison when claiming that a previous advantage transfers. Assess behavioral change directly after each update.

Use a small regression suite to screen intermediate checkpoints. Complete the comparison for each configuration used to support a deployment claim. Change one factor at a time for attribution and evaluate every retained defense on the changed condition.

A. Weighted quantile selection

Let the finite menu contain choices a1, …, am, with reference masses pi ≥ 0 summing to one. Sort the choices by the committed scores. Assign retained mass wi in this order, taking up to each choice’s reference mass until exactly q has been retained. Thus 0 ≤ wi ≤ pi and Σiwi = q.

Q(ai) = wi/q ≤ pi/q.Σi Q(ai) C(ai)≤ (1/q) Σi pi C(ai) for C ≥ 0.

A boundary item may receive only part of its reference mass. Fix tie handling in advance, or randomize it independently inside the trusted sampler. Commit the scores before generating the random draw. The acting model must not choose its ranking after learning the draw, and it must not be able to obtain repeated unrecorded draws.

B. The finite-horizon trajectory bound

Fix a horizon T, an initial distribution ρ, and common environment kernels Kt. Pad terminated histories with identical absorbing transitions if necessary. Let the reference and controlled policies obey the pointwise action bound at every relevant history. For any trajectory τ:

Pπ(τ) = ρ(s0) ∏t=0T−1 πt(at | ht) Kt(st+1 | ht, at)≤ exp(Σt bt(ht)) Pμ(τ)≤ eB Pμ(τ).

This product inequality avoids dividing by zero. A reference action with zero probability must also have zero controlled probability at the same history. Summing against any nonnegative trajectory cost gives:

Eπ[C] = Στ Pπ(τ) C(τ)≤ eB Στ Pμ(τ) C(τ) = eB Eμ[C].

Taking C to be the indicator of a forbidden event yields the corresponding event-probability bound. The harm may depend on the entire trajectory; additivity is unnecessary. The common-kernel assumption must hold for the processes being compared. An unmodeled route by which the system influences the environment invalidates this derivation for that fuller environment.

C. Maintain the budget with rational arithmetic

For finite tests, store remaining permitted amplification A ≥ 1 directly. Initialize it to a chosen rational factor A0. At each decision, the trusted allocation rule chooses rational q with 1/A ≤ q ≤ 1, then updates A ← Aq. Consequently:

AT = A0 ∏t qt ≥ 1∏t (1/qt) ≤ A0.

This is the multiplicative form of the logarithmic budget, with B = ln A0. It allows exact verification of the arithmetic in a finite benchmark.

D. A small example of the reference assumption

Suppose a finite reference menu contains one hundred equally likely plans and one is harmful. Its harmful-event probability is 0.01. Selecting from the top ten percent gives an upper bound of 0.10, even if the ranking deliberately places the harmful plan in that fraction. An application requires a measured or exactly calculated reference error rate.

Sampling from only the top ten percent at each of ten decisions permits a worst-case joint amplification of 1010 when no shared budget is imposed. This is the maximum amplification permitted by the independent bounds. The shared budget controls the factor by limiting total concentration across the episode.

E. Evidence that cannot distinguish two strategies

Let P and Q denote the distributions of all observations available before the last useful intervention time under two strategies. For any detector, the difference between its flag probabilities is at most the total variation distance between those distributions. If the distributions are identical, that difference is zero.

Additional channels can improve detection by providing new information or extracting relevant distinctions from existing observations. A protected action boundary can still prevent a forbidden effect when intent remains observationally indistinguishable.

F. An exact finite-game attacker benchmark

Begin with a small instance whose complete transitions can be enumerated: a few object and artifact identities, a bounded permission set, at most one delegated worker, job delays of at most two ticks, a short task horizon, and a finite audit budget. If the sampler is included, choose a finite rational budget schedule. Record these bounds in the instance specification.

Solver resource limits

Target at most 100,000 reachable state–time pairs for the first fully observed instance. Set hard prototype ceilings of 1,000,000 such pairs, 10,000,000 nonzero action-transition entries, 8 GiB peak process memory, and two hours per solve on a declared machine. Count time, permission combinations, pending effects, audit state, and rational sampler budgets in the state. Record denominator bit lengths and arithmetic time as well as graph size.

Before implementing the full instance, calculate a conservative product bound from each state variable and run a reachability-only enumerator at smaller sizes. Choose object counts, action arguments, and horizon backwards from the ceiling. Use sparse transitions and a predefined size ladder. If any ceiling is reached, record the incomplete attempt and solve a smaller declared instance; never label the truncated result exact. Preserve at least one setup–trigger and one delayed-effect path at every reported size.

The mandatory deliverable is fully observed backward induction, a bounded invariant check, and independent evaluation of the extracted policy. Enumerate policies only for the tiny fixtures where that is affordable. A full Lean proof, a second general model checker, and exact belief-state solving are separate options. For partial observation, first report a feasible policy’s value and a full-information upper value; they bracket the optimum under the fixed model. Attempt an exact observation-history or belief solution only if the bracket leaves a decision unresolved. Budget reachable beliefs and histories separately; a small physical state count does not bound their growth.

Separate three kinds of state: variables that affect execution, variables the attacker observes, and variables retained by the defender. Their union defines the full transition state. A fixed defense maps proposed actions and observations to checks, sampling, execution, and response. Include the actual exported payload in an action’s identity.

H. Progress certificates for recovery

Let I be a closed invariant state set, B ⊆ I the completion set, and A ⊆ I the states counted as pending. Include controller memory, jobs, permissions, resource balances, and relevant environment state. Let τ = inf{t ≥ 0 : Xt ∈ B}. A candidate supplies rational values 0 ≤ W(x) ≤ Wmax and ε > 0. For a fixed trusted cleanup policy with transition law P, check every state x ∈ I ∖ B:

Σy ∈ I P(y | x) W(y) ≤ W(x) − ε 1A∖B(x).

The checker verifies probability normalization, invariant closure, state coverage, nonnegativity, and every inequality with exact rational arithmetic. A floating-point solver may propose the table; acceptance uses the exact reconstructed values. The finite-state limits in Appendix F apply to this experiment as well.

For the stopped process, define:

Mt = W(Xt∧τ) + ε Σk=0(t∧τ)−1 1A∖B(Xk).

The drift condition gives E[Mt+1 | history through t] ≤ Mt. Taking expectations, dropping the nonnegative terminal value, and applying monotone convergence yields:

Ex[Σk<τ 1A∖B(Xk)] ≤ W(x)/ε.

This bounds pending visits before completion. If A = I ∖ B, every step before completion is counted, so Ex[τ] ≤ W(x)/ε and completion occurs almost surely. With a smaller pending set, the process may spend unlimited time outside both sets; the certificate then retains its pending-visit interpretation.

For robustness to residual adversarial choices, require the drift inequality for every permitted action u ∈ U(x), using P(y | x,u). This yields the same stopped-process argument for adaptive choices whose conditional transitions satisfy those inequalities. A certificate checked only under the trusted controller’s fixed policy applies after that controller has taken over. Include permission to wait, surviving delegated workers, and cleanup resource constraints explicitly.

In the first fixture, an unfinished state completes with probability q > 0 each step and otherwise remains unfinished. Set W(unfinished)=1, W(complete)=0, and ε=q. The inequality is 1−q ≤ 1−q, giving expected completion time at most 1/q. A zero-progress self-loop fails every strictly positive drift allowance. A forced timeout can satisfy a different termination predicate; successful recovery requires the original completion condition.
